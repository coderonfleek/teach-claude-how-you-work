# I Tried Time Blocking for 30 Days. One Block Survived.

At 9:40 on a Tuesday morning in December, my calendar said "Deep work: draft" in a calm blue block. I was on the kitchen floor with a roll of paper towels, because Biscuit had come back from our walk, found the one puddle on our street, and then walked it straight across the tiles.

That was day two of a 30-day time-blocking experiment. By the time the floor was clean, half the blue block was gone, and the next one ("Admin, 45 min") was already sitting in the sidebar looking smug.

I'd read the same advice you probably have: give every hour a job and the hours stop wandering off. So from 1 to 30 December I blocked my whole working day in Google Calendar and wrote down, every evening, which blocks actually happened. What I found was smaller than I expected, and more useful.

## The setup

Every Sunday evening I planned the week. Each working day got six blocks: writing (9:00–10:30), admin (10:45–11:30), editing (11:30–12:30), lunch and a walk, research (14:00–15:30), and a "catch-up" block at the end of the day.

Each block had its own colour. Writing was blue, admin grey, editing green. I spent 50 minutes the first Sunday choosing those colours, which was the most productive-feeling and least productive hour of the month.

Every evening I wrote three lines in a note: how many blocks I kept, which ones broke, and why. A block counted as "kept" if I started within 10 minutes of the planned time and did the thing it said.

## What the numbers said

Across 20 working days I planned 120 blocks. I kept 48 of them. That's 40%, or about 2.4 blocks a day.

But the blocks didn't fail evenly. The morning writing block survived 16 of the 20 days. The afternoon research block survived 4. Admin did fine, mostly because admin expands to fill whatever slot you give it, so it often "kept" itself during time that was meant for editing.

The number I cared about most was words. In the four weeks before the experiment I averaged about 3,100 draft words a week. During it I averaged 4,700. Almost all of the extra came out of the 9 a.m. block.

## The one idea

Time blocking didn't work for my whole day. It worked for one block.

The writing block survived because it sits right after the morning walk, before anything has happened that needs a reply. By 9:00 Biscuit is asleep on the rug, my partner has left for work, and my inbox hasn't woken up yet. The block wasn't fighting anything.

The afternoon blocks were fighting everything. A note from Dan about an edit, an interview that ran 20 minutes over, a parcel, a dog who has decided 2:30 is when a second walk is owed. Every one of those was reasonable. Together they turned my research block into a nice blue rectangle I looked at while doing something else.

It's like meal prepping. If you make Monday's lunch on Sunday night, you'll eat it. If you make Friday's too, by Friday it's sad and you're buying a sandwich. The plan was fine. It was just too far from the moment it had to hold.

So in week three I stopped pretending. I kept the 9 a.m. block as a fixed appointment and turned the rest of the day back into a short list. I also stopped ending each day staring at a calendar full of things I hadn't done, which did more for my mood than any colour scheme.

## What didn't work

**Blocking the whole day.** Six blocks a day meant six chances to fail. On the worst day, a Thursday in week one, I kept zero. I'd planned it perfectly and lived it differently.

**Colour-coding.** The colours made the calendar look lovely and told me nothing. I never once looked at a green block and felt more like editing.

**Buffers.** In week two I added 15-minute buffers between blocks. They got eaten by whatever the previous block hadn't finished, so every block still started late, just in a more organised way.

**The catch-up block.** A block called "catch-up" is a block that admits the plan won't work. On 17 of the 20 days I did nothing in it except feel bad about the other blocks.

**Blocking evenings.** In week one I blocked 20:00–21:00 for reading. My partner saw it on our shared calendar and asked whether dinner now needed a booking too. I deleted it that night.

## What I do now

It's January and I still have exactly one block: writing, 9:00–10:30, Monday to Friday, blue. It's a recurring event with no description, because I know what it's for.

Everything else lives on a paper list next to my laptop. If something on the list needs real time, I give it a block that same morning, not on Sunday night. Planning closer to the moment seems to be most of the trick.

I've kept the morning block on 6 of the 7 working days since the experiment ended. The one I missed was a dentist appointment, which I'm choosing to count as a different kind of calendar success.

## Try this week

Pick one 90-minute block at the time of day when the fewest people need you. Put it in your calendar for three days this week. Don't block anything else.

At the end of each of those days, write down one thing: did the block happen, yes or no. On Friday, look at your three answers. That will tell you more about whether time blocking suits you than a fully coloured calendar ever did for me.
