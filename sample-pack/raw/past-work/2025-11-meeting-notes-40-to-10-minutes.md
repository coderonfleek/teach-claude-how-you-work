# How I Cut My Meeting Notes From 40 Minutes to 10

At 4:15 on a Thursday afternoon in October, I finished tidying my notes from a 32-minute interview and looked at the clock. The notes had taken 41 minutes. Biscuit was asleep on my feet, my tea had gone cold twice, and I'd spent longer writing about the conversation than having it.

That week I had seven calls: four interviews for articles, a check-in with Dan, a planning call with a writer friend, and a co-working session that somehow always ends with a to-do list. I timed my notes after each one. The average was 40 minutes. The total was 4 hours 38 minutes, which is more than half a working day spent turning talking into typing.

So I spent the next two weeks trying to get that number down. It's now about 10 minutes a call. The fix was smaller than I expected, and it wasn't really the AI tool. It was what I asked it for.

## Where the 40 minutes went

My old process: record the call (with permission), get an automatic transcript, then read the transcript and write notes by hand. Reading a 30-minute transcript took me about 15 minutes. Writing notes from it took another 20. Then I spent 5 more minutes deciding where to put them.

None of those steps felt slow on its own. That's the annoying thing about admin. Nobody ever blocks 40 minutes for "notes" in their calendar. It just leaks out of the hour after the call, and then you wonder why the afternoon disappeared.

The obvious move was to let an AI do the summary. So in week one I pasted each transcript into a chat assistant and typed "summarise this call".

It did. The summaries were fine. They were also 450 to 700 words long, written in a polite, even tone that made every topic sound equally important. I still had to read each one carefully to find the three things I needed. My average in week one was 31 minutes. Better, but not by much.

## Ask for what you'll do, not what was said

A summary answers "what happened?" I didn't need that. I was there.

What I needed after every call was the same three things: what we decided, what I have to do, and what's still open. Everything else was the scaffolding of the conversation.

It's like coming home from the supermarket. You don't need a summary of the trip ("entered via the fruit aisle, considered the oat milk"). You need to know what's in the bags, what you forgot, and what you'll have to go back for.

So in week two I changed the prompt to this:

> From this transcript, list only: 1) decisions we made, 2) things Maya has to do, with any dates mentioned, 3) questions nobody answered. Use the speakers' own words where possible. If a section is empty, write "none".

The answers came back at 80 to 150 words. I could read them in two minutes, check them against the transcript in five, and move them into my notes in one or two. My average for week two was 10 minutes across six calls. The fastest was 6 minutes (Dan's check-in, which is mostly him saying "fine, ship it"). The slowest was 15.

The shorter output also changed how I read it. With a 600-word summary I skimmed, which meant I missed things. With three short lists I actually read every line, because there were only about a dozen of them.

## What didn't work

**The invented deadline.** In week two the AI told me I'd promised an interviewee a draft "by Friday". I hadn't. Nobody said Friday. I only caught it because I check the to-do list against the transcript every time, and that's the one step I'd never skip now. It's half of my 10 minutes, and it's worth it.

**Recording everything.** I asked to record the planning call with my writer friend, and the whole thing went stiff. We both started talking like we were on a podcast. Now I only record interviews, where a transcript earns its keep, and jot three quick lines by hand for everything else.

**Short calls.** For anything under 15 minutes, uploading the recording and checking the output took longer than writing the three headings myself. My cut-off is now 20 minutes.

**"Use the speakers' own words."** This helped for decisions and hurt for questions, which sometimes came back as half-sentences like "but what about the, um". I left it in anyway. A messy real quote is easier to check than a tidy invented one.

## Where the remaining 10 minutes go

About 2 minutes reading the three lists, 5 minutes checking the to-dos against the transcript, and 3 minutes moving things to where they belong: to-dos onto my task list, interview quotes into the article outline in Notion.

Across the seven calls in the first week of November, my total notes time was 1 hour 12 minutes. Same number of calls as that October week, about three and a half hours less typing. I've mostly spent it on a longer morning walk, and Biscuit has raised no objections.

## Try this week

After your next call that runs longer than 20 minutes, don't write a summary. Open a blank note and write three headings: Decided, I'll do, Still open. Fill them in from memory, or from a transcript if you have one.

Time it. If it's quicker than your usual notes, do it for every call next week. Only then think about getting an AI to fill the headings in for you.
