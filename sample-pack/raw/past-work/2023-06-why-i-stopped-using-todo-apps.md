# Why I Stopped Using To-Do Apps (And What I Do Instead)

Have you ever spent more time organising your to-do list than actually doing anything on it?

If you're nodding, you're in good company. I have personally downloaded, set up, colour-coded, abandoned and then felt guilty about more to-do apps than I'd like to admit. At one point I had three of them on my phone at the same time, each holding a slightly different version of the same list. None of them were finished. Some of them contained the same task, "sort out to-do apps", which tells you everything.

So a few months ago I deleted all of them. Here's why, and what I use now.

## The problem isn't you. It's the app.

To-do apps are brilliant at one thing: adding tasks. They make it so easy. You have a thought in the shower, you open the app, you type "email accountant", you feel organised. Lovely.

What they're terrible at is the other half: deciding what actually gets done today. Every task looks the same size in a list. "Email accountant" sits right next to "rethink entire career" in the same font, with the same little empty circle. Your brain has no idea which one to start with, so it starts with neither and goes to make a cup of tea.

Over time the list just grows. Mine got to well over a hundred items. It stopped being a plan and became a sort of museum of good intentions. I'd open it in the morning, feel a wave of dread, and close it again.

## Features are not productivity

Every app I tried had a new feature that was going to fix everything. Tags. Priorities. Projects inside projects. Due dates that repeat. A calendar view. A board view. A view of the board view.

And every time, I'd spend a happy Sunday afternoon setting it all up. Which felt exactly like work. It just wasn't the work.

I genuinely think some of us use to-do apps the way some people use gym memberships. Paying for it feels like doing it.

## The guilt adds up

This is the part nobody talks about. An app that holds a hundred unfinished tasks is an app that tells you, every single morning, that you're behind.

You're not behind, by the way. You just wrote down more things than any human could do in a month. But the app doesn't know that, and after a while neither do you.

When I finally deleted mine, the main feeling wasn't loss. It was relief. Like taking off shoes that had been a size too small for three years.

## What I do instead

It's almost embarrassingly simple.

Every morning, I take one index card. I write down three things I'm going to do today. Not ten. Not "top priorities plus stretch goals". Three.

If something else comes up, it goes on a scrap piece of paper on the side, which I look at once a week and mostly throw away. You'd be amazed how many "urgent" things stop being urgent when you leave them alone for six days.

At the end of the day, the card goes in the bin, whether I finished all three or not. Tomorrow gets a new card.

That's it. No sync, no notifications, no premium plan. The whole system costs about four pounds a year in index cards, and my dog Biscuit has eaten roughly a third of that budget.

## "But what about big projects?"

Fair question. Big projects still exist. I keep them in a single notes document, one line each, and I look at it on Monday mornings to decide which three-thing cards they'll show up on that week.

The trick is that the project list is not the to-do list. It's the shelf. The card is the plate. You only eat what's on the plate.

## Is this for everyone?

Probably not. If you manage a team of twenty people with dependencies and deadlines, you might genuinely need software. That's fine. Keep it.

But if you're like I was, one person with a list that never ends and an app that never stops reminding you about it, try a week without it. You might find the list was the problem all along.

Because at the end of the day, it was never about the perfect app.

**Stop managing your tasks. Start finishing them.**
