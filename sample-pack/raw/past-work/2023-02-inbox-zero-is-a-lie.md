# Inbox Zero Is a Lie

Is there anything more satisfying than an empty inbox?

That little illustration your email app shows you when there's nothing left. A sun, maybe, or a cheerful mountain. "You're all caught up!" For about four minutes, you feel like the most organised person alive.

I chased that feeling for years. I archived, I filtered, I snoozed. I read every article about it and set up folders inside folders. And I've come to a conclusion that might be unpopular: Inbox Zero is a lie. Not a malicious one. More the kind of lie you tell yourself at 11 p.m. when you say you'll just answer one more email.

Here are three reasons why.

## 1. It never stays at zero

This is the obvious one, but it's worth saying out loud. Email is not a pile you can finish. It's a river.

You can spend an hour getting your inbox to zero, and by the time you've made a coffee to celebrate, there are six new messages. Two of them are newsletters you don't remember signing up for. One is a reply to an email you sent to get to zero in the first place.

Getting to zero is like mopping the kitchen floor while the dog is still coming in from the garden. Technically possible. Never finished. (I say this as someone whose dog has very strong opinions about mud.)

When your goal is a number that resets itself every few minutes, you'll never feel done. And never feeling done is exhausting.

## 2. It rewards the wrong kind of work

Here's the sneaky part. Clearing your inbox feels productive. You're making decisions, moving things around, replying quickly. Your brain gets a little reward every time a message disappears.

But most of what's in there isn't your real work. It's other people's requests, notifications, updates, receipts. Clearing it is a lot like tidying your desk before starting a project. Useful, sometimes. But if you do it every day for two hours, you'll have a very tidy desk and no project.

I realised this when I looked back at a week where I'd hit Inbox Zero four days out of five. I'd felt brilliant all week. I had also written almost nothing. The emails were done. The work wasn't.

## 3. It puts email in charge of your day

The biggest problem with Inbox Zero is that it makes your inbox the boss.

If zero is the goal, then every new email is a small job that's been added to your day. Someone else gets to decide what you work on, just by pressing send. And because the inbox is always there, always one tab away, you end up checking it constantly, just in case zero has become one.

Your day becomes a series of reactions. You never quite get to the thing you actually wanted to do, because there's always one more message between you and it.

I once tracked how often I opened my email in a single working day. I gave up counting somewhere after lunch, which is its own kind of answer. Every one of those checks felt harmless. Added together, they were the shape of my whole day.

## So what instead?

I'm not saying ignore your email. Some of it matters, and people will eventually notice if you don't reply.

What helped me was changing the goal. Instead of "empty inbox", I aim for "inbox checked on purpose". Twice a day, late morning and late afternoon, I go through it properly. I reply to what needs replying, archive what doesn't, and leave the rest. Then I close the tab.

The first week felt wrong, like leaving the house without checking the oven. By the second week I'd stopped noticing. By the third, I'd written more than in the whole month before. Funny how that works when you stop spending your best hours on other people's to-do lists.

Some days there are forty emails sitting there when I log off. The world has not ended. Nobody has written to complain. (Well, one person did, but by email, so I saw it the next morning.)

The empty inbox illustration is lovely. I've just stopped needing to see it every day.

**Your inbox is not your to-do list. Stop treating it like one.**
